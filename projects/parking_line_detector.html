Parking Line Detector

A computer vision algorithm for detecting parking lines in around-view monitoring images, designed for real-time operation in autonomous parking systems.

April – May 2024 C++ · Computer Vision · ADAS

Overview

Accurate recognition of parking lines is a fundamental part of autonomous parking systems. This project presents a computer vision approach to recognizing parking lines using an around-view monitoring (AVM) system — a synthesized top view of the vehicle built from front, rear, left, and right cameras.

With real-time performance in mind, the method operates on two smaller regions of interest within the surround-view image rather than the full frame, and tracks detected lines across frames using vehicle odometry. The goal was to build something simple, efficient, and adaptable to diverse environmental conditions — without requiring complex sensor configurations.

Why Around-View Monitoring?

Working with AVM images has three key advantages for this problem. Only a single image needs to be processed, parking lines appear as straight lines (rather than foreshortened by perspective), and any position in the top-view image can be mapped directly to a relative distance from the vehicle — which is exactly what a path-planning system needs.

The catch is that AVM images become less reliable as distance from the vehicle increases, due to occlusions and reduced pixel resolution. To address this, the algorithm operates on smaller regions of interest near the left and right cameras, then relies on vehicle movement to track recognized lines over time.

Preprocessing

A major challenge in parking line detection is separating genuine parking lines from pavement cracks, shadows, and lighting variations. The key observation here is that pixels corresponding to cracks and shadows typically have lower intensities than the average pixel intensity in the image.

The algorithm exploits this by computing the average pixel intensity of the region of interest and replacing any pixels below that average with the average value. This simple step effectively eliminates pavement cracks and significantly reduces the impact of shadows, without needing any environment-specific tuning.

The image is then smoothed with a 3×3 median blur, which was chosen over a Gaussian blur because the AVM images contained more salt-and-pepper noise than typical Gaussian noise.

Edge Detection and Endpoint Extraction

After preprocessing, the Sobel operator is applied to compute edge magnitudes and gradient directions. Each gradient is then rounded to one of four orientations — 0°, 45°, 90°, or 135° — since parking lines have a rectangular shape, and their sides emit edges with similar gradient directions.

To suppress noise, three filtering rules are applied:

  • Suppress edges that are weaker than some percentage of the strongest edge.
  • Suppress vertical edges — parking lines cannot appear vertical relative to the vehicle.
  • Suppress edges whose gradient direction differs from their immediate neighbours along the orthogonal direction.

The result is a refined edge map that makes line search both faster and more reliable. Endpoints are then extracted by starting at each non-suppressed edge, travelling in the orthogonal direction (along the edge), and continuing as long as the subsequent edges share the same direction. A small gap tolerance allows the search to skip over brief interruptions, such as a line partially occluded by a curb, before terminating. The final line must exceed a minimum length to be accepted.

Tracking with a Circular Buffer

Detected lines in a single frame are unreliable on their own — they can be partial, spurious, or noisy. To stabilize detection across frames, I implemented two circular buffers, one for left-side parking lines and one for right-side lines, each holding the N most recent detections.

Between frames, the buffers are updated using vehicle odometry: each tracked line is shifted by the vehicle's horizontal and vertical movement and rotated by its change in heading. The buffers also maintain running statistics — average start point, end point, and direction vector — which serve two purposes. First, they allow partial detections to be extended to their expected length, filling in occluded sections. Second, they help filter false positives by enforcing a minimum distance between detected lines.

Results

The final algorithm reliably detects parking lines across a range of environmental conditions, including pavement cracks, shadows, and varied lighting. Because it operates only on small regions of interest and tracks detections over time rather than re-detecting from scratch each frame, it runs in real time on the target hardware.

The methodology and experimental results were documented in a technical paper as part of the project.

Reflections

This was the project where I first experienced the gap between a textbook algorithm and a working system. Edge detection and Hough transforms are standard tools, but applying them to real AVM footage meant thinking carefully about which pixels matter, how to distinguish parking lines from pavement cracks, and how to keep detection stable across frames even when individual frames were noisy.

Working under a real-time constraint also shaped the design considerably. Every decision — operating on small ROIs, using a circular buffer for tracking, filtering edges before searching for lines — came from the requirement that the algorithm be fast enough to run continuously. It was a good reminder that in applied computer vision, the interesting engineering problems often start where the textbook algorithms end.
