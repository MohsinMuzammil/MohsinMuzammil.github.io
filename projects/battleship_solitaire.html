Battleship Solitaire

Solving the Battleship Solitaire puzzle as a constraint satisfaction problem with a ship-based formulation, forward checking, and arc consistency.

November 2024 Python · Artificial Intelligence · Constraint Satisfaction

Overview

In this project, I tackled the Battleship Solitaire puzzle by formulating it as a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP). The puzzle presents a grid with hidden ships, and the goal is to deduce the locations and orientations of those ships from row and column constraints, along with partial information about the ships that has already been revealed.

The puzzle is constrained in several ways: by ship size, by placement rules (ships cannot touch each other, even diagonally), and by the number of ship parts that must appear in each row and column. Solving it requires propagating these constraints intelligently rather than blindly searching the space of possible board configurations.

A Ship-Based Formulation

Most implementations of Battleship Solitaire treat each cell as a variable. I opted for a different approach: I modeled each ship as a variable instead. This minimizes the number of variables and constraints, better aligns with the rules of the game, and leads to more efficient constraint handling.

The trade-off is that the search space per variable is larger — each ship has many possible placements — but the constraints between ships are far more natural to express. Rather than encoding "this cell cannot contain two ships" as a constraint on every pair of cells, I encode "these two ships cannot overlap" as a constraint on two variables.

This formulation captured the full complexity of the puzzle in a way that felt more intuitive and more manageable, even though it required more careful thinking upfront.

Preprocessing the Board

A significant portion of my time went into preprocessing the board to extract as much information as possible before the main search began. By identifying water cells and known ship parts early, I could systematically deduce the presence of complete ships — reducing ambiguity and streamlining the problem setup.

This preprocessing step greatly enhanced the efficiency of the subsequent search. Every ship I could place or eliminate during preprocessing was one fewer variable the solver had to reason about later.

Constraint Propagation

To solve the puzzle, I implemented a CSP solver using backtracking search, supported by forward checking and arc consistency (AC-3) to propagate constraints efficiently.

I also incorporated Generalized Arc Consistency (GAC) alongside the Minimum Remaining Values (MRV) heuristic, which prioritizes the most constrained variable at each step. Additionally, I optimized the constraint queue by ordering constraints according to a custom heuristic, further accelerating the search.

One particularly useful optimization was a custom function to check for Domain WipeOut (DWO) scenarios before invoking GAC's hasSupport() method. This allowed the algorithm to detect invalid search paths earlier and avoid significant wasted computation.

Results

Fastest in class one of the most efficient CSP solvers in the course

The final solver reliably solves Battleship Solitaire puzzles of the size and complexity used in the course, and my implementation was among the fastest in the class.

Reflections

This project presented several challenges, requiring extensive troubleshooting and refinement to ensure the puzzle was solved correctly and efficiently. While many would have chosen the simpler cell-based formulation, I was determined to tackle the more complex ship-based approach because it would lead to a more efficient and elegant solution.

That decision paid off. The ship-based model captured the problem more accurately and led to a solver I'm proud of. Along the way, I developed a much better intuition for constraint propagation, search heuristics, and the kind of small implementation details — like the DWO check before hasSupport() — that can turn a correct solver into a fast one.
