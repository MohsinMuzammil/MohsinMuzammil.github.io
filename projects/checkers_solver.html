Checkers Endgame Solver

An AI solver for Checkers endgames using alpha-beta pruning, successor generation, and transposition tables.

October 2024 Python · Artificial Intelligence · Game Tree Search

Overview

In this project, I implemented an AI solver for Checkers endgames in Python using alpha-beta pruning. The solver navigates the game tree, evaluating states to find optimal moves for both players, and is designed to solve endgame puzzles in the fewest possible moves while assuming both sides play optimally.

The solver handles simple moves, mandatory jumps, and multi-jump sequences, using an evaluation function and optimizations like node ordering and state caching to improve search efficiency.

The Heuristic

I initially experimented with several heuristics — distinguishing between kings and normal pieces, analyzing piece layouts, and looking for patterns. But the simplest heuristic turned out to be the most effective for endgames: evaluating the difference in piece count between the two players.

In the endgame, when very few pieces remain on the board, the material advantage almost always determines the outcome. A more complex heuristic added overhead without improving decisions, and in some positions actually made the search worse by favoring patterns that didn't matter.

Successor Generation

A key part of the implementation was the design of the generate_simple_successors() and generate_jump_successors() functions. These functions generate the legal moves from a given board state, adhering strictly to the game's movement rules.

In generate_simple_successors(), movement directions are determined by the type of piece: normal pieces move forward diagonally, while kings can move in any diagonal direction. Simple moves are handled in a single pass with minimal branching.

generate_jump_successors() was more involved. Jumps can be mandatory, and they can chain into multi-jump sequences that must be fully resolved before the turn ends. Rather than solving this with recursion, I implemented it iteratively using a stack to track in-progress jump sequences. This avoids recursion overhead and cleanly handles all jump scenarios — single jumps, mandatory jumps, and multi-jumps — in a single framework.

Search Optimizations

To make the Minimax search practical on real puzzles, I layered two optimizations on top of the base alpha-beta pruning algorithm:

  • State caching with a transposition table. Different move orders often lead to the same board state. By caching the evaluated value of each unique state, I avoided re-searching subtrees that had already been explored.
  • Successor ordering. Before exploring a node's successors, I sorted them by their heuristic value so the most promising moves were examined first. This maximizes the number of alpha-beta cutoffs, since good moves near the top of the ordering are more likely to prune the rest of the subtree.

Results

Optimal play solves endgame puzzles in the fewest moves, with efficient search even on complex scenarios

The solver reliably finds optimal move sequences on the endgame puzzles used in the course, even for complex multi-jump scenarios. Combined, the optimizations above make the search fast enough to explore large game trees without sacrificing correctness.

Reflections

This project gave me a much deeper understanding of game tree search — not just how alpha-beta pruning works in theory, but how its performance depends on the details: heuristic quality, successor ordering, and state caching.

It also reinforced something I keep noticing across projects: a simpler solution is often the better one. The elaborate heuristics I tried first were outperformed by piece count. The recursive multi-jump approach I drafted first was cleaner and faster as an iterative stack-based version. In both cases, understanding the problem well enough to simplify it was more valuable than adding sophistication.
